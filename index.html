When the Call Sounds Real

The Scenario

It's a Tuesday afternoon. A member of your team receives a call from someone who identifies themselves as an IT technician. The caller says they've detected suspicious activity linked to the team's shared account and that credentials must be reset within the hour — or access will be suspended for the entire department.

"I'm calling from the IT helpdesk. We've flagged an unusual login from your account. I need you to verify your current password so I can push the reset before the system locks tonight. It'll take two minutes."
Step 1 First Contact — The Moment You Receive the Call
The call just came in. You've been on the phone 30 seconds. The caller sounds calm, professional, and uses internal terminology. You haven't confirmed or denied anything yet.
Discuss with your team (or think through yourself)
  • What specific red flags, if any, do you notice in the caller's language?
  • Would the urgency framing ("before the system locks tonight") affect your response — and why?
  • What would you say to the caller in this moment, and what would you NOT say?
  • Does the fact that they know your name and team make them more credible?
Step 2 Verification — Confirming Identity Before Acting
You've decided not to give any information over the phone. The caller insists they're legitimate and says: "I can give you a callback number so you can confirm the ticket with my manager."
Discuss with your team (or think through yourself)
  • Why is the callback number the caller provides potentially compromised?
  • Where would you independently find the real IT contact number?
  • How do you end the call politely without revealing anything further?
  • What would you tell your manager immediately after hanging up?
Step 3 After the Call — Escalation and Reporting
You've ended the call. You verified with the real IT team using a number you found independently: no one called you. The attack is part of a wider campaign targeting multiple people in your organisation.
Discuss with your team (or think through yourself)
  • Who do you notify first — your manager, IT, or the security team?
  • What specific information should your incident report contain?
  • How do you alert other team members who may receive the same call?
  • Should you tell anyone outside the organisation? Why or why not?

Key takeaways from this scenario
Never give credentials over the phone
Urgency is a pressure tactic, not a reason
Verify independently — not via the caller
Report even when you said no
Real IT never needs your password
Colleagues may be targeted simultaneously
Social media makes names and teams findable
Your report protects the whole team
🌐 The Odido Incident — February 2026

Attackers used phone impersonation, urgency framing, and internal language to access Odido's customer database. 6.39 million records were stolen. No malware was used — the entire attack worked through a single phone call.
